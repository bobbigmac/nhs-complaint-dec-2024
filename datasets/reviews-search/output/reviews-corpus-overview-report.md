# What The Review Corpus Shows

This note looks across the whole indexed Google review corpus, not just access.

The corpus used here contains `16,293` plaintext reviews across `165` practices. It is a local fulltext read of the review text, using the new reviews search tool. It is not a survey and it is not NLP. It is patients speaking in their own words, often about one sharp experience.

That matters. Google reviews are not representative in the clean statistical way a patient survey tries to be. They are self-selecting, emotional, and uneven. But that is also why they are useful. They show where care breaks down badly enough, or works well enough, that people feel they have to say so in public.

## The Big Shape

The first thing the review corpus shows is how polarised it is.

- `6,129` reviews, `37.6%`, are `1` star
- `475` reviews, `2.9%`, are `2` stars
- `365` reviews, `2.2%`, are `3` stars
- `928` reviews, `5.7%`, are `4` stars
- `8,396` reviews, `51.5%`, are `5` stars

So the middle is tiny. Patients mostly do not write like measured survey respondents. They write when they feel badly let down, or when they feel someone really helped them.

That split is visible at practice level too. `157` of the `165` practices in this corpus have both low-star and high-star reviews. So the picture is rarely just "good practice" or "bad practice". A lot of practices look unstable or uneven from the patient side. Some people feel well cared for, while others describe being blocked, ignored, or badly handled.

The GTD-managed slice is harsher. In that subset, `627` of `830` reviews, `75.5%`, are `1` or `2` stars, while only `185`, `22.3%`, are `4` or `5` stars. That is a much more complaint-heavy pattern than the wider corpus.

## What Patients Mostly Complain About

### Access still dominates

Access is still the biggest single story in the dataset.

Using the access basket from the earlier report, `5,028` reviews, `30.9%` of the whole corpus, are low-star reviews that hit a main access problem. Broad access language appears in `8,680` reviews, `53.3%` of all reviews.

That means access is not just one topic among many. It is the front door through which many other complaints arrive.

But it is not the only thing patients are saying.

### Staff attitude and respect

One of the clearest non-access themes is how patients talk about the way they are spoken to.

`1,765` low-star reviews, `10.8%` of all reviews and `26.7%` of low-star reviews, hit a staff-attitude bucket with words like `rude`, `attitude`, `hostile`, `unhelpful`, `arrogant`, or `disrespectful`.

These are not just complaints about politeness. In the review text, tone often comes bundled with power. Patients describe reception or staff behaviour as part of being blocked, doubted, brushed off, or made to feel small.

Examples:

> "Very rude, unprofessional reception staff"  
> Beth Richmond, `Mandalay Medical Centre`, `1` star

> "Firstly, the staff receptionist were incredibly rude and unhelpful."  
> Saad farooqi, `Kingsway Medical Practice`, `1` star

> "the admin/reception team here are something else, I totally agree with every complaint made against them in the reviews"  
> Hafsa Bakari, `New Bank Health`, low-star review

This is one of the clearest ways the review corpus goes beyond the patient survey. The survey can ask whether reception was helpful. Reviews show what "not helpful" looks and feels like in practice.

### Admin follow-through, results, referrals, prescriptions

Another strong theme is not just getting through the door, but what happens after that.

`1,071` low-star reviews, `6.6%` of all reviews and `16.2%` of low-star reviews, mention referrals, results, prescriptions, callbacks, letters, or related admin follow-through problems.

Patients often describe this as drift, loss, or having to chase. These reviews are less about a single rude moment and more about systems that feel loose, unreliable, or hard to trust.

Examples:

> "did not ring me although waiting blood and scan results"  
> nicola cooper, `Dr Y Loomba & Partner`, `1` star

> "lost blood test results ... and I found out 10 month later this referral that gp has suppose sent never was sent"  
> rachel barnes, `Droylsden Road Surgery Branch`, `1` star

> "I never knew it would be so difficult to get a repeat prescription for Omeprazole."  
> Rosemary Mallace Overfiftyfitness, `Brooklands Medical Practice`, `1` star

This matters for representation because the survey usually slices this into separate neat questions. The reviews show how patients experience it as one thing: not knowing whether the practice will actually carry something through.

### Clinical trust, being listened to, and feeling safe

A smaller but still important theme is clinical trust.

`707` low-star reviews, `4.3%` of all reviews and `10.7%` of low-star reviews, hit a bucket around diagnosis, medication, treatment, or negligence-type language.

This is where the stakes feel highest. Patients are not just frustrated. They are saying they do not trust what they were told, what they were prescribed, or whether someone really listened.

Examples:

> "Be careful with this practice, misdiagnosed my uncle after months of going back and forth."  
> Ste Minator, `RADCLIFFE MEDICAL PRACTICE`, `1` star

> "misdiagnosed a communicable infection - the WORST practice I have ever had to attend."  
> Ruth Whittaker, `The Alastair Ross Medical Practice`, `1` star

> "I've had a terrible time at this practice, I've been misdiagnosed, I've had results that weren't reported to me and it is an impossible task to book an appointment."  
> RobAlexander, `Bolton Community Practice CIC - Ladybridge Surgery`, `1` star

The survey does ask some narrow questions about whether a healthcare professional listened or gave enough time. Reviews show what those failures can mean when patients feel the cost of them.

## What Patients Praise

The positive side of the corpus is also clear, and it is useful because it shows what good care looks like in patient language.

### Helpful, caring, listening clinicians

`4,418` high-star reviews, `27.1%` of all reviews and `47.4%` of high-star reviews, mention helpful, caring, kind, listening, reassuring, professional, thorough, or compassionate care.

That is a very strong signal. When patients are happy, they often do not just say "good service". They say someone listened, took them seriously, reassured them, or sorted something properly.

Examples:

> "Extremely helpful and caring doctors. Always feel listened to and well looked after."  
> Tahreem F, `Ailsa Craig Medical Centre`, `5` stars

> "The doctors are all very helpful and again excel in your treatment I feel safe and listened to at this practice."  
> Mick Edgerley, `Deane Medical Centre`, `5` stars

> "A very knowledgeable practitioner who listened and was very caring."  
> Helen Smith, `The Poplars Medical Practice`, `5` stars

### Friendly and welcoming front desk staff

`2,118` high-star reviews, `13.0%` of all reviews and `22.7%` of high-star reviews, praise staff as friendly, welcoming, respectful, or lovely.

That is the mirror image of the low-star reception problem. Reception is not a side issue in either direction. It is one of the biggest ways patients decide whether a practice feels safe, usable, and human.

Examples:

> "Friendly staff on reception and helpful"  
> caroline daniels, `RK Medical Practice`, `5` stars

> "Excellent practice with friendly and helpful reception staff."  
> Matthew Delooze, `Bodey Medical Centre`, `5` stars

> "Staff in reception are super helpful and friendly."  
> River Area, `Bolton Medical Centre`, `5` stars

### Easy and quick access when it works

`1,866` high-star reviews, `11.5%` of all reviews and `20.0%` of high-star reviews, mention access positively with words like `easy`, `quickly`, `prompt`, or appointment-related praise.

That matters because it shows that patients do notice access when it works well. Good access is not invisible. They often talk about fast answers, easy appointments, and staff getting back to them quickly.

Examples:

> "I've found it quite easy to get an appointment. Staff are all great and get back to you quickly."  
> Cailen, `Wilbraham Surgery`, `5` stars

> "always answer the phone quickly and it's easy to get an appointment"  
> Sean Doherty, `The Birches Medical Centre`, `5` stars

> "I've always been so impressed with how quickly I get an appointment/ an Answer."  
> Ellie L, `Barlow Medical Centre`, `5` stars

## How Patients Write About Problems

The reviews do not read like survey responses. They read like people describing what happened to them.

Some recurring features stand out:

### 1. Patients write in chains, not categories

Survey questions split problems into neat boxes: phone contact, appointment offered, overall experience, healthcare professional listened.

Reviews do not do that. Patients usually tell a sequence:

1. could not get through
2. finally got through
3. was told nothing was left
4. was pushed to go online
5. got no response
6. felt spoken to badly
7. then had to chase results or referrals

That chain is one of the most important differences between the review corpus and the survey.

### 2. The language is plain and often hard-edged

Patients do not soften much. Common low-star words include `appointment`, `phone`, `staff`, `doctor`, `told`, `rude`, and `receptionist`.

They use simple judgment words:

- rude
- useless
- disgusting
- awful
- unhelpful
- appalling
- disgrace

That plain language is important. It tells you how people interpret the system, not just what happened in a formal sense.

### 3. Patients often name the point of failure

They talk about:

- the phone line
- the receptionist
- the form
- the referral
- the repeat prescription
- the specific GP

That makes the review corpus much more operational than it first looks. It gives clues about where the service feels brittle from the patient side.

### 4. Positive reviews are just as concrete

Good reviews are not just "great service". They often say:

- I was listened to
- they got back to me quickly
- reception were welcoming
- the doctor was reassuring
- someone sorted the problem

So the review corpus is good at showing what patients want, not only what they hate.

## Where Reviews Go Beyond The Patient Survey

The national patient survey is useful, but its interests are narrow.

It tends to ask things like:

- was it easy to contact the practice?
- did you know what the next step was?
- were you offered a choice?
- did the healthcare professional listen?
- was your overall experience good?

Those are useful gateway questions. But the review corpus shows at least five things the survey cannot show well.

### 1. Reviews show the route, not just the score

The survey tells you whether contact felt easy. Reviews tell you how patients tried:

- phone
- online form
- website
- callback
- repeat prescription route

And they show where that route broke.

### 2. Reviews show emotional cost

The survey records dissatisfaction. Reviews show humiliation, anger, panic, confusion, exhaustion, and mistrust.

That matters if the goal is to represent patient need clearly. A system that looks merely "fairly difficult" in survey language can look unbearable in review language.

### 3. Reviews show people who may not have made it through the formal pathway

This is one of the biggest gaps.

Survey responses are often shaped by people who got far enough through the system to answer about their contact or appointment experience. Reviews include people describing being blocked at the door, bounced between routes, or giving up after repeated failed attempts.

That means reviews are especially valuable for seeing exclusion and drop-off.

### 4. Reviews join service stages together

The survey separates access, appointment quality, support, and overall experience. Reviews show how these interact.

Patients often describe bad access, rude handling, and weak follow-through as one continuous failure, not three separate ones.

### 5. Reviews show what good care feels like

The survey can tell you if people say a professional listened. Reviews tell you what that means in real life:

- not feeling rushed
- being reassured
- being taken seriously
- someone going above and beyond
- a receptionist sorting something instead of deflecting it

That is useful if the aim is not only to criticise, but to describe what patients are actually asking for.

## What We Need To Know To Represent Patients Better

If this review corpus is used to speak more clearly for patients than the survey usually can, a few points matter most.

### 1. Access is only the start

Access is the biggest issue, but patients do not stop there. They also talk about respect, clarity, follow-through, prescriptions, referrals, and whether they trust what they were told.

So any representation that only says "appointments are hard to get" will miss a lot.

### 2. Reception is not just an admin layer

In the reviews, reception is often the face of the practice. It is where patients feel helped, blocked, believed, doubted, respected, or dismissed.

That makes reception behaviour a patient-need issue, not just a staffing issue.

### 3. Patients want both kindness and competence

The positive reviews are not only about warmth. They are about people who are kind and actually get things done.

The negative reviews are not only about delay. They are about delay plus confusion, plus being spoken to badly, plus not trusting that the next step will happen.

### 4. Mixed practices need closer reading

Because most practices have both high and low reviews, the real question is often not "is this place good or bad?" It is:

- who gets through
- who gets stuck
- when does the service work
- where does it break
- which failures keep repeating

### 5. The survey should be read with the reviews beside it

The survey is better for broad comparison.

The reviews are better for:

- patient language
- repeated front-door failures
- named operational problems
- emotional cost
- the difference between being in the system and being shut out of it

Taken together, they give a fuller picture than either on its own.

## Bottom Line

Across the board, this review corpus says three big things.

First, the patient experience is sharply polarised. Many people leave very happy. Many others leave very angry. Very few sit in the middle.

Second, access is the biggest theme, but not the only one. Patients also keep returning to staff attitude, weak follow-through, prescriptions and referrals, and whether they feel listened to and safe.

Third, the reviews show patient need in a form the survey usually cannot: direct, concrete, event-based, and emotionally clear.

If the aim is to represent patients more clearly, the review corpus is useful not because it is tidy, but because it is messy in exactly the ways lived experience is messy. It shows the routes patients try, the points where they get stuck, the language they use when they feel shut out, and the language they use when care genuinely works.
