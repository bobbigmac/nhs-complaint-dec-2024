Author: Tom Caton

Rating: 1 star
PART 1: I’m not one for knocking the health services normally, but when someone close has had a bad experience I guess the need is there to speak up. A family member, who would prefer to remain nameless went into the surgery earlier today with an issue that had previously saw him see a doctor immediately; receive injections (in the surgery) due to what was potentially a life-threatening condition and have the doctor immediately call an ambulance for him. However, earlier on today the madam behind the counter took it upon herself to declare that said family member would not be seeing a doctor, in what would by all accounts sound like a ‘greater-than-thou’ voice of authority. We all know the type. The one’s who let a little bit of power go to their head. The one’s who think they’re being forthright and accurate but sound like complete and utter swines. Of course, diplomatically speaking, tough decisions are part and parcel of these jobs and the folk in that role will no doubt rise and fall based on their decisions. However, we’re not talking about a landlord’s decision as to whether someone drinks in their pub or a shopkeeper’s decision as to who is served on their premises. These are lives we’re dealing with. Surely any doubts should be cast aside even if it’s in the ‘just in case’ category. But Cruella’s actions had consequences which meant that a situation which could have made my family member’s day a lot less stressful turned into a bit of a nightmare which ended up with him in hospital, subjected to numerous tests and (at present) at least one overnight stay. You could argue these things would have happened anyway, but a little bit of consideration, kindness and thought would have made things much easier. Would it have been too difficult to call for a doctor, just in case? Just for a second opinion? I highly doubt it. The arrogant so and so who made the call: Does she have the medical knowledge to send someone packing and so nonchalantly tell them to call 111 or at best go straight to A&E, especially after she was advised of what happened the previous time such a thing occurred? Would a ‘better safe than sorry’ approach have been more appropriate? It’s easy stamping down your authority, being direct when you’re in that position, but where’s the empathy? The compassion? The commitment to the patient? Maybe it will take a worse outcome next time for the receptionist to change her attitude, but next time it might be too late for the person on the receiving end of her authoritative ways. If she isn’t already, then the woman should feel thoroughly ashamed at the way she dealt with the situation today. I just hope she doesn’t make a wrong call in future which could have dire consequences. PART 2: Monday: After an injury over the weekend, I call for an appointment. I know what you’re thinking- idiot. Yes. I don’t get through. Fill in online form for sick note. Message says turn around is 3 working days. (Fair enough) Call Monday afternoon to make sure this has been received. Told it has been and the turn around is usually 48 hours, so should be ready by Wednesday. (Fine, a day earlier than expected) Call Wednesday afternoon to be told it wasn’t ready but should be by tomorrow. (No worries) Call Thursday and speak to someone who sounds very helpful, telling me I’m fifth in the queue, she expects it to be dealt with by end of her shift and she takes my email to forward it to me. (Great! We’re getting somewhere.) Call reception Friday morning to be told it was unlikely the sick note would be with me today and the previous person had got it wrong (not ideal) Call reception Friday afternoon to be told a doctor would be calling me on Tuesday. This person was thereafter offended and annoyed by me daring to question why it would take six working days to arrange for a sick note to be signed off. She thought it appropriate to talk over me, interrupt me, and show not a scintilla of patience for the patient (Great that I eventually got an answer but overall absolutely appalling).
