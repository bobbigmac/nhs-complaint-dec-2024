# Digital Access Failures – Raised December 2024, Escalated August 2025

## Patient Participation Group Talking Points - Appointment System Concerns

I’d like to discuss a problem with the appointment system at New Bank Health Centre that’s making it unnecessarily hard to get care. If a doctor can’t reach a patient by phone, the request is deleted outright, and they have to resubmit it the next day from scratch. This strips patients of control over their access to care for reasons like work, poor signal, or just missing a call. It’s frustrating, unfair, leads to patient-blaming instead of helpful outcomes, and I think it can be improved.

## Key Points to Raise

1. **Unpredictable Availability**

   - Patients can’t wait by the phone all day for an unscheduled call. Jobs, family, or technical issues (e.g., no signal) mean missing a call isn’t their fault.
   - **NHS Link**: NHS guidelines say appointments should be reasonable and consider patients’ circumstances.

2. **Deleted Requests Create Barriers**

   - Deleting requests after missed calls forces patients into a daily resubmission loop, delaying care with no guarantee of success.
   - **NHS Link**: The NHS Constitution calls for equitable access to care, but this system penalizes patients unfairly.

3. **Vulnerable Patients Suffer Most**

   - Elderly or disabled patients may struggle with the online form or being available for calls, risking exclusion from care.
   - **NHS Link**: NHS accessibility rules require alternative methods for those who can’t use digital systems.

4. **Misleading "Missed Appointment" Label**

   - Calling an unscheduled call a "missed appointment" isn’t accurate and might inflate statistics, affecting trust and policies like charging for no-shows.
   - **NHS Link**: Accurate appointment data is vital per NHS guidance—mislabeling distorts this.

5. **No Notification Worsens the Problem**

   - Patients aren’t always told when their request is deleted (I wasn't for 3 of the 4), leaving them confused and wasting time checking manually.
   - **NHS Link**: NHS England stresses clear communication from the first contact—patients deserve to know their request status.

## Solutions to Suggest

- **Don’t Delete Requests**: Keep appointment requests open for a set period (e.g., 48 hours) even if you rejected it, or ask the patient if they want to close it or reopen tomorrow automatically.
   - You don’t have to make multiple call attempts—we get time is tight—but 2 calls inside 20 seconds isn't giving patients time to spot a missed call to keep an eye out for another call, so it's no different from 1 call practically.
   - Patients should at least be able to reschedule (or even better escalate to non-phone path).
- **Schedule Call Windows**: Let patients pick a time range for callbacks to fit their lives.
- **Offer Alternatives**: Add phone or in-person booking for those who can’t use the online form. Your patient survey suggests 40% of people are not making it through your system for even a first appointment.
- **Notify Patients Reliably**: Send a text or email if a request is closed (which ideally it wouldn't be), with clear next steps. Likewise for support messages, which just black hole currently.
- **Have Some Empathy/Humanity**: People come to the doctor rarely, when they need help most... Most people are not as tech savvy as you, and certainly do not have comfortable devices for filling out long relatively complex forms multiple times or every day. When the receptionist treats people badly because it's technically the patient's own fault, it just drives them further away from your help.
- **Don't Make Doctors Call People**: A simple live-holding system where the Doctors review and queue requests, but a staff member makes the calls, and queues them into the Doctor as needed, would improve the process on both ends, with more flexibility and efficiency for everyone involved.s


## **CORE ISSUES**
- **Digital-only booking system** - no phone/in-person options
- **Unscheduled callbacks** - patients wait all day with no timeframe
- **Two-strikes deletion policy** - missed calls = deleted requests
- **No same-day resubmission** - must wait until next day
- **Misleading terminology** - "missed appointments" for unscheduled calls
- **Tiny text fields** insufficient space for entering detailed medical information
- **Poor notifications** - requests deleted without notification

## **IMPACT ON PATIENTS**
- **Vulnerable groups excluded** (elderly, disabled, digitally excluded)
- **Patient safety risks** - delays in care access
- **Mental health impact** - uncertainty and anxiety
- **Time wasted** - all-day waiting for uncertain outcomes
- **Statistical misrepresentation** - inflates "missed appointment" figures

## **EVIDENCE FROM REVIEWS**
- **40+ complaints** about appointment system since 2017
- **Average rating 1.5/5** for appointment-related complaints
- **Consistent themes**: long phone waits, confusing digital system, rude staff
- **Pattern**: Better Health MCR management (post-2017) shows significant decline

## **NHS POLICY VIOLATIONS**
- **Patient communication standards** - unclear notifications
- **Accessibility requirements** - no alternative booking methods
- **Data accuracy** - mischaracterizing missed calls as appointments
- **Patient rights** - equitable access not provided

## **ESCALATION PATH**
1. **Practice Manager** (direct complaint)
2. **Healthwatch Manchester** (0300 078 0669)
3. **NHS England** (0300 311 22 33)
4. **NHS Counter Fraud Authority** (if intentional misrepresentation suspected)

## **PROPOSED SOLUTIONS**
- **No Deletion** The patient didn’t magically recover. They reached out for help, and you deleted their request—sometimes with a dismissive, blame-shifting response.
- **Alternative booking methods** (phone/in-person)
- **Scheduled callback windows** instead of all-day waiting
- **Flexible missed call policy** - allow same-day resubmission
- **Clear notifications** when requests are closed
- **Accurate terminology** - don't call unscheduled calls "appointments"

## **BROADER IMPLICATIONS**
- **Systemic issue** - the doctors might be great, but some patients don't get the chance to find out
- **Potential fraud** - inflated missed appointment statistics
- **Public interest** - worth investigating across Manchester practices
- **Journalistic angle** - could escalate to Manchester Mill/Press

## A Diplomatic Training Challenge

*Issued to management*

From Patient Complaints to Real Fixes: A Short Worksheet for Managers. This worksheet might stop us reaching for “more training” every time something goes wrong. Think of it as a quick check under the bonnet: what’s actually broken, and what’s just rattling because it’s loose?

**Learning Objective**
By the end of this module, managers will be able to:

- Spot when ‘training’ is skipping the diagnosis of the cause.
- Link actions to root causes, not appearances.
- Use plain, accurate language that supports change.”

**Background**
A recent patient participation meeting and many (many) practice reviews have highlighted concerns about staff *incompetence*. When summarised diplomatically as *“training challenges”*, these issues risk being misrepresented and therefore mis-solved.

**Case Study**

- Patient complaint: “Staff are incompetent.”
- Management summary: “We face training challenges.”
- Standard solution: Issue refresher PDFs or brief training sessions.
- Result: Visible activity; little evidence of behaviour change or outcome improvement.

**Root Cause Analysis**
Possible drivers of the behaviour (beyond knowledge/skill):

- Burnout from prolonged pressures (e.g., five years of pandemic).
- Depression, disengagement, or loss of agency in the workplace.
- Structural constraints leaving staff unable to help patients, leading to avoidance behaviours (silence, hanging up, labelling patients as “problematic”).
- Not simply lack of knowledge or skill.

**Learning Checkpoint**
Q: Which of the following can be solved with training PDFs?
<ol type="a">
  <li>Forgetting how to use a system function</li>
  <li>Being exhausted and disengaged after years of impossible workloads</li>
  <li>Aggressive silence on patient calls</li>
  <li>A lack of clear authority or agency within the practice</li>
  <li>Frustration with troubled patients and ejecting them instead of helping them</li>
  <li>An absence of ongoing support from senior and similarly stressed staff</li>
  </ol>

(Answer: a)

**Key Takeaway**
Calling incompetence a “training challenge” minimises the issue and 'locks in' the wrong fix. Management should ensure that language reflects reality, so that solutions are more likely to address root causes, not appearances.

**Next Steps for Managers**

- Resist diplomatic minimisation when recording patient concerns.
- Link solutions explicitly to original complaints.
- Measure whether implemented solutions change patient outcomes.
- Verify response was effective with a simple before/after measure (e.g., call completion rate, patient callbacks, complaint category trend)

# When the GP Patient Survey can’t “see” the patients who didn’t get access

The NHS GP Patient Survey (GPPS) is supposed to be a national feedback loop: a way for patients to describe what access and care felt like, and for practices, regions and the NHS to spot problems and fix them. It’s run by Ipsos on behalf of NHS England. citeturn1view1

But if your main concern is **access failure** — digital-only gates, “submit a form” systems that close, phone queues that never connect, unscheduled callbacks that get missed and then silently close your request — the survey has a built-in weakness: **the people most harmed are systematically harder to measure, and the instrument often compresses “no access” into “a bit difficult”.**

That’s how a tool intended to uncover harm can end up **hiding its real scale**.

---

## Who designs and governs this survey

NHS England commissions it and Ipsos operates it. citeturn1view1turn1view0

The official GPPS FAQ says the questionnaire was originally developed with the University of Exeter and University of Cambridge, is reviewed regularly, and changes are tested using cognitive interviews. citeturn1view0

The GPPS technical annex describes a steering group (since 2014) that debates questionnaire content and analysis/reporting, with representation including DHSC, CQC, Healthwatch England, patient reps, a PPG rep, and others. citeturn2view0

So there’s no mystery “single author”. This is a governed national instrument with multiple points where problems *could* be corrected.

---

## The hidden denominator problem: “survivorship bias” in survey form

A lot of access harm happens **before** a patient successfully enters the practice’s “journey”:

- the form is closed / capped
- you’re told “call back tomorrow”
- you’re in a queue that never answers
- you miss an unscheduled call and your request is closed
- the only routes require tools/time you don’t have (work hours, disability needs, language, low confidence online)

If the survey questions and reporting mostly capture people who *made it through*, the numbers look calmer than reality.

That’s not a conspiracy. It’s just measurement design: the denominator quietly shifts from **“everyone who needed help”** to **“everyone who successfully engaged enough to answer this”.**

---

## Where the questionnaire can’t represent reality

### 1) “Difficult” replaces “impossible”

The three opening access questions are framed as *ease/difficulty* scales:

- “Generally, how easy or difficult is it to contact your GP practice on the phone?”
- “…using their website?”
- “…using the NHS App?”

The only “escape” option is “I haven’t tried”. There is no “I tried but could not contact them / it was closed / I was blocked”. fileciteturn4file8

So if access is frequently *denied*, you’re forced into answers that imply the service exists but is merely fiddly.

### 2) The reporting layer often discards the “escape” option anyway

The analysis tool explicitly states for Q1–Q3: “Patients who selected ‘I haven’t tried’ have been excluded”, and shows an “Excluding those who said ‘I haven’t tried’ …” base. fileciteturn4file3

That matters because “I haven’t tried” is exactly where digital exclusion, learned helplessness, fear of wasting time, and repeated prior failure often end up.

If you exclude that group from headline summaries, you’re excluding the signal.

### 3) Answer sets top out before “never happened”

Some timing questions cap at “after two or more days / can’t remember”, but don’t allow “it never happened” (no next step ever arrived; nobody followed up; it disappeared). fileciteturn4file8

When “never got a response” doesn’t exist as an answer, the instrument nudges people into softer, more ambiguous categories — or into self-blame (“can’t remember”).

### 4) “Preference” language assigns patients a choice they didn’t have

Your dentistry example is the cleanest illustration:

“Why haven’t you tried to get an NHS dental appointment in the last two years?” includes “I prefer to go to a private dentist.” fileciteturn4file8

If the real-world state is “no NHS dentists nearby are taking new patients”, the offered answer reframes constraint as preference. It’s not just missing reality; it replaces it.

## Response rates: who is easiest to miss

Even a well-run survey can struggle to capture people who are time-poor, demoralised, digitally excluded, or repeatedly bounced.

GPPS 2025 reports an overall (unadjusted) response rate of **25.8%** (702,837 returned from 2,721,415 sent). citeturn3view0

But the same section shows response rates vary sharply by age: for example **8.5% (18–24)** and **10.6% (25–34)**. citeturn3view0

That doesn’t prove “people don’t answer because the survey is useless” (that’s harder), but it does support a key point: **large groups are lightly represented**, and those groups overlap strongly with modern access friction (work hours, online-first, unstable housing, language, mental health, etc.).

## What would make the survey fit-for-purpose for access harm

If GPPS is meant to measure access and inequality, it needs to capture *blocked states* directly and report them, not file them away.

Minimal fixes that would change everything:

1. **Add “I tried but could not” options** to the early access questions (phone/website/app), distinct from “I haven’t tried”. fileciteturn4file8
2. **Stop excluding “I haven’t tried” from summaries**, or at least publish it as a first-class indicator (because it’s often the harm signal). fileciteturn4file3
3. Add explicit outcomes like **“I never received a next step / no response”** where timing questions currently force soft answers. fileciteturn4file8
4. Replace “preference” answers with **availability/constraint** answers where services are structurally scarce (dentistry being the obvious one). fileciteturn4file8
5. Pair survey results with **operational access metrics** that can’t be gamed by wording: attempts, blocks, closures, unanswered calls, missed-callback closures, and time-to-first-human-response.

That last one is important: a survey will always miss some people. Operational metrics help prevent the polite fiction that “no data = no harm”.
